# Negotiation Techniques (research notes)

Attribution: mikesmayer/claude-business-skills, negotiation/SKILL.md (https://github.com/mikesmayer/claude-business-skills/blob/main/negotiation/SKILL.md), after Chris Voss, Never Split the Difference. The Forge draft restates the method in its own words and adds the Forge rules: a written brief before round one, a round record after each round, and a BATNA attached to every position.

## The eight techniques in the source

1. Tactical empathy: consciously imagine yourself in the counterpart's situation, then vocalize their perspective to create trust. Empathy is not agreement.
2. Mirroring: repeat the last 1 to 3 critical words with a curious upward inflection, then go silent (4+ seconds).
3. Labeling: identify and verbalize emotions with "It seems like", "It sounds like", "It looks like". Naming a negative emotion diffuses it; naming a positive one reinforces it.
4. Calibrated questions: open "How" and "What" questions that shape the conversation while giving the counterpart the illusion of control. "How am I supposed to do that?" is the strongest pushback without saying no. Avoid "Why", which sounds accusatory.
5. Accusation audit: before negotiating, list and preemptively verbalize every negative thing the counterpart might think or say about you.
6. "That is right": summarize the counterpart's position so accurately they respond with "That is right". "You are right" is dangerous; it usually means dismissal. Use the Rule of Three: confirm agreement 3 times in 3 different ways.
7. Ackerman bargaining: calculated offers in decreasing increments (65 percent, 85 percent, 95 percent, 100 percent) with precise non-round numbers and a non-monetary bonus at the end.
8. Black Swans: hidden, game-changing pieces of information; about three lurk in every negotiation. Types: secret constraints, hidden motivations, unknown context. Three kinds of leverage surface them: positive (what they want), negative (what they fear), normative (their own stated values).

## Counterpart styles in the source

- Analyst: methodical, data-driven, hates surprises. Use facts, be patient, do not rush.
- Accommodator: friendly, relationship-focused, avoids conflict. Build rapport, but pin down specifics.
- Assertive: direct, time-conscious, wants to win. Be efficient, stand firm, acknowledge their points.

## Common mistakes in the source

Splitting the difference; pushing for "yes" (produces counterfeit agreement); arguing your position; skipping the accusation audit; countering an extreme anchor immediately; using "Why"; treating "You are right" as success.

## What the Forge draft takes from this

The technique set and the scoring idea (the source rates a negotiation 0 to 10 against full tactical empathy engagement; the draft turns that into a verification checklist instead of a score). The draft does not copy the source's tables or copy patterns. It adds the Forge rules: preparation brief, round record, BATNA per position, and no terms agreed without operator approval of the exact text.
